### **Lecture Overview: The Learning Problem**

This lecture serves as an introduction to machine learning (ML), outlining its core components, the mathematical framework, and different types of learning. The lecturer emphasizes that machine learning is a broad subject ranging from abstract theory to practical rules of thumb.

---

### **1. The Essence of Machine Learning**

Machine learning is applicable when three specific conditions are met:

1. **A pattern exists:** There is an underlying relationship to be discovered (e.g., how people rate movies).
2. **We cannot pin it down mathematically:** The relationship is too complex to be defined by a simple closed-form equation or code.    
3. **We have data:** Historical data is available to learn from.


**Example Case: Movie Ratings (Netflix Prize)**

- **Goal:** Predict how a viewer will rate a movie based on historical data.
- **Approach:** Instead of manually defining factors (like "comedy content" or "viewer preference for comedy"), the system reverse-engineers these factors from the data.
- **Mechanism:** The algorithm starts with random factors and iteratively adjusts them to match existing ratings until the factors become meaningful predictors.

---

### **2. Formalizing the Learning Problem**

The lecturer uses a "Credit Approval" metaphor to formalize the components of learning.

#### **Components of the Framework:**

- **Input ($x$):** The data used for prediction (e.g., customer attributes like salary, debt).
- **Output ($y$):** The decision or result (e.g., approve/deny credit, $+1$ or $-1$).
- **Target Function ($f$):** The ideal, unknown formula that perfectly maps $x$ to $y$. This is what we want to approximate.
- **Data ($D$):** A set of historical examples $(x, y)$ generated by the target function.
- **Hypothesis ($g$):** The final formula produced by the learning algorithm that approximates $f$.

#### **The Learning Solution:**

- **Hypothesis Set ($H$):** The set of all possible candidate formulas the algorithm can choose from (e.g., linear models, neural networks).
    
- **Learning Algorithm:** The method that searches through $H$ to find the hypothesis $g$ that best fits the data $D$.
    
- **The Learning Model:** The combination of the Hypothesis Set and the Learning Algorithm.
    

---

### **3. First Learning Model: The Perceptron**

The lecturer introduces the **Perceptron** as a simple linear model for binary classification.

- **Hypothesis Form:** It calculates a weighted sum of the inputs. If the sum exceeds a threshold, the output is $+1$ (approve); otherwise, it is $-1$ (deny).
    
    - Mathematically: $h(x) = \text{sign}(w^T x)$, where $w$ is the weight vector and $x$ includes an artificial coordinate $x_0 = 1$ to handle the threshold.
        
- **Perceptron Learning Algorithm (PLA):**
    
    - Iteratively updates the weights $w$ to correct misclassified points.
        
    - **Update Rule:** If a point is misclassified, update the weight vector: $w \leftarrow w + y_n x_n$. This nudges the vector closer to the correct classification.
        
    - **Convergence:** If the data is **linearly separable**, PLA is guaranteed to eventually converge to a solution that classifies all training points correctly.

### **4. Types of Learning**

The lecture categorizes learning based on the nature of the data and supervision.

- **Supervised Learning:**
    
    - **Definition:** The training data includes both the input and the correct output (labels).
        
    - **Example:** Coin recognition where the coins are labeled with their denominations (25¢, 10¢, etc.).
        
    - **Usage:** This is the most common type and the focus of the course.
        
- **Unsupervised Learning:**
    
    - **Definition:** The data consists only of inputs; there are no target labels.
        
    - **Example:** Grouping coins into clusters based on size and mass without knowing their values, or learning a language by just listening to the radio.
        
    - **Goal:** To find structure or higher-level representations in the data.
        
- **Reinforcement Learning:**
    
    - **Definition:** The system does not receive the correct output but gets a "grade" or feedback (reward/punishment) on its actions.
        
    - **Example:** A toddler learning not to touch a hot cup (pain = negative reward) or a computer learning to play Backgammon by winning or losing games.
        
    - **Mechanism:** Credits or penalties are propagated back to previous actions to adjust behavior over many trials.
        

---

### **5. The Learning Puzzle & Feasibility**

The lecture concludes with a puzzle to illustrate a fundamental theoretical challenge.

- **The Puzzle:** Given a small set of labeled examples (patterns of black/white squares), students are asked to predict the label of a new, unseen pattern.
    
- **The Outcome:** The audience splits between predicting $+1$ and $-1$.
    
- **The Lesson:** Both answers are technically correct because, without further assumptions, the unknown target function could be _anything_ outside the observed data.
    
- **Implication:** Pure memorization is not learning. For learning to be feasible (to generalize to new data), we need to establish a theory that allows us to make guarantees about the unknown target function based on finite data. This leads into the next lecture on the theory of learning.
