## Summary: Introduction to Digital Image Processing

### Overview

- **Definition of a Digital Image**: An image is defined as a two-dimensional function, f(x,y), where the coordinates (x and y) and the amplitude (intensity or gray level) are all finite, discrete quantities.
    
- **Digital Image Processing**: This refers to the processing of these digital images using a digital computer. The individual elements of a digital image are most commonly referred to as "pixels".
    
- **Beyond Human Vision**: While human vision is limited to the visual band of the electromagnetic (EM) spectrum, imaging machines can cover almost the entire spectrum, from gamma waves to radio waves, and even use sources like ultrasound and electron microscopy.
    

### The Scope of the Field

The continuum of computerized image processes can be broken down into three levels:

- **Low-Level Processes**: Primitive operations where both inputs and outputs are images, such as noise reduction, contrast enhancement, and image sharpening.
    
- **Mid-Level Processes**: Tasks where the input is an image, but the output consists of extracted attributes (e.g., edges, object recognition, and image segmentation).
    
- **High-Level Processes**: The cognitive functions associated with computer vision, which involve "making sense" of the recognized objects in an image.
    

### Historical Origins

- **Early Transmission**: One of the earliest applications was the Bartlane cable picture transmission system in the 1920s, which reduced the time to send pictures between London and New York from over a week to less than three hours.
    
- **Tied to Computers**: True digital image processing began when computers became powerful enough in the early 1960s.
    
- **Space Program**: Early major applications included processing images from space probes, such as NASA's Ranger 7 mapping the moon in 1964.
    
- **Medical Advancements**: The invention of computerized axial tomography (CAT/CT scans) in the early 1970s was one of the most significant milestones for image processing in medical diagnosis.
    

### Application Areas by Energy Source

Images are categorized by their energy source, predominantly utilizing the electromagnetic spectrum:

- **Gamma-Ray Imaging**: Extensively used in nuclear medicine (such as bone scans and PET scans) and astronomical observations (like capturing the Cygnus Loop).
    
- **X-Ray Imaging**: Used for medical diagnostics (chest X-rays, angiography, and CAT scans), as well as industrial inspections (e.g., finding flaws in electronic circuit boards) and astronomy.
    
- **Ultraviolet Imaging**: Applied in areas like fluorescence microscopy to study materials and diseases (like identifying infected crops) and in lithography.
    
- **Visible and Infrared Imaging**: The most broadly used bands. Applications include light microscopy, remote sensing (e.g., NASA’s LANDSAT satellites monitoring environmental changes and weather phenomena like Hurricane Katrina), automated visual inspection in manufacturing, and law enforcement (fingerprint processing, automated license plate reading).
    
- **Microwave Band**: Primarily used for radar imaging, which has the unique advantage of collecting data regardless of weather or ambient lighting conditions.
